Excellent question to nail down, because it's the mechanics everything else stands on. Let me trace it concretely with one of your apps — say LDCWTH — through before, during, and after.
First, the basics of how any app uses a QM. An application never talks to another application directly, and it never "holds" messages itself. It opens a client connection to a queue manager — addressed by hostname + port + channel (in your OpenShift setup, each QM pod has a Kubernetes Service, so the app's env vars hold something like qm-shared-2.roco-dev.svc:1414). Once connected, the app works purely with queue names: the producer opens LDCWTH.REQ.Q and PUTs messages to it; the consumer connects and GETs from the same name. The queue is where messages rest; the QM is the post office that owns the queue. The app knows two things only: which QM to connect to and what the queue is called.
Before — the 9 shared sources. Your apps (LDCWTH, RO, ZN, and the rest) are scattered across those 9 QMs, with multiple apps' queues co-located. So QM_SHARED_2 might host LDCWTH's queues and RO's queues. LDCWTH's producer connects to QM_SHARED_2's service and PUTs to LDCWTH.REQ.Q — a QLOCAL sitting right there; its consumer connects to the same QM and GETs from it. RO's traffic flows through the same pod. That co-location is the shared blast radius: QM_SHARED_2 has a bad day, both apps have a bad day.
During migration — why both generations run at once. The dedicated pod QM_LDCWTH comes up with LDCWTH.REQ.Q defined as a QLOCAL on it. The bridge goes in: transmission queue + sender channel on QM_SHARED_2, receiver on QM_LDCWTH. Then the cutover: on the source, the QLOCAL LDCWTH.REQ.Q is replaced by a QREMOTE definition of the exact same name, pointing at QM_LDCWTH via that transmission queue. Now here's the key: the producer still connects to QM_SHARED_2 — same DNS, same port, the env vars you show in the demo — and still PUTs to the same queue name. But when MQ resolves that name, it finds a remote definition instead of a local queue, so it drops the message onto the XMITQ and the channel carries it to the target's QLOCAL. The producer is sending messages to a queue manager it has never heard of, through a connection it never changed. Meanwhile the consumer reads from the target side — and the messages that were already sitting on the source's local queue at cutover get drained down to your proven zero.
After — the 7 dedicated targets. LDCWTH's traffic now lives entirely on QM_LDCWTH: producer PUTs, consumer GETs, same queue names throughout. Once validation passes and the source queues are empty and removed, the app's connection can be pointed at the dedicated QM directly — and in Kubernetes there's an elegant way to do even that invisibly: re-target the Service DNS name to the new pod, so the connection string in the env vars never changes either. The 9 sources exist only until every tenant has left them; with all 7 apps migrated, they drain, get decommissioned, and steady state is the 7 dedicated QMs. That's why the fleet is 16 during the exercise and 7 at destination — the 16 is a snapshot of an estate mid-flight.
The compressed version if Tiago asks: "Apps connect to a queue manager by address and work with queues by name. Before: many apps' queues on one shared QM. The cutover swaps the app's local queue for a same-named remote alias, so the same PUT to the same name on the same connection now rides an MQ channel to the dedicated QM. After the drain, the app's whole world is its own queue manager — and it never knew it moved."
